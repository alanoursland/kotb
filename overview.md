# The Caves of Chaos, Reconsidered

*A reframing of B2: The Keep on the Borderlands as a campaign about hunger, legitimacy, and the people a society pushes to the bottom — set in the Grand Duchy of Karameikos, Mystara.*

## The core idea

The classic module hands you a dungeon: seven caves of "evil monsters," sorted by species, good for nothing but converting into experience points. But read the text closely and Gygax accidentally wrote something else. Each lair has a population, leaders, dependents, weapons, treasure, alliances, and feuds. The kobolds keep to their warren. The factions watch each other and sometimes cooperate. They don't fight constantly — they maintain a working détente in marginal terrain. The label says "Chaos." The behavior says **settlement.**

So the premise is simple and it changes everything: **take the behavior seriously and let the labels fall away.** The Caves of Chaos aren't a monster pit. They're a multi-ethnic frontier community — poor, hungry, locked out of the trade networks the Keep controls — doing what desperate people do when the road is the only thing between them and starving.

The raiding isn't malice. It's subsistence. *Hungry is the whole explanation.*

## The pitch

Duke Stefan Karameikos is enlightened — or wants to be. He understands comparative advantage. He suspects the humanoid peoples of his frontier have real, specialized value (kobold engineering, hobgoblin discipline, gnoll labor, the strength of ogres and bugbears) and he doesn't believe they're irredeemably evil. But he also can't let the violence on his roads continue.

He hires the player characters to **open diplomatic and trade relations with the Caves of Chaos** — and to clear them out if they won't even talk. *The wilderness will be tamed one way or another.* That open hand with a closed fist behind it is the moral content of the whole commission. The Duke is sincere, and he will still issue the order. Holding both at once is what makes him interesting.

The campaign is not "will the party kill the monsters." It's: **can a fragile, multi-party federation be built and fed before the things circling it tear it apart?**

## What makes it work

**The labels never track anything.** Sort every creature by three questions instead of alignment: *Is it a society? Does it live by preying on the group you're asking it to join? Does it produce anything but negation?* Suddenly the roster resolves cleanly — integrable polities, a couple of genuinely hard cases, some wildlife, a few opted-out individuals, a human banditry problem that exposes the lie in the words, and exactly one irreducible evil. (The published prisoner tables prove the point on their own: the freed orc fights then flees like any POW, one freed human is simply a murderer, captivity drives a gnoll mad. Species predicts nothing. Circumstance predicts everything.)

**Every disqualifying behavior in the caves has a human institution doing the same thing with better paperwork.** The hobgoblins eat captives; the bugbears keep slaves — and the Duke harbors the Black Eagle Baron, a human slaver who keeps his title. The road-raiders the party is sent to stop are mirrored by a human raider camp nobody calls "monsters." The party's recurring discovery is that the line between "person" and "monster" was always drawn by people invested in drawing it.

**It's an economy, not a morality play.** Peace pays better than predation the moment someone builds the market. You don't moralize the caves out of cannibalism or slaving — you make those behaviors *uneconomical* by giving people a better trade. Self-interest does the rest, the same way it does for everyone.

## The cast (a sampling)

- **Brundt the ogre** — a defense-biased mercenary whose venality has accidentally kept the peace; the swing vote nobody can cheaply buy. First contact, first easy "yes." Goes to the Keep, gets a bath and a bed, civilizes himself the instant a market exists for it — and discovers his counterfeit coins are fake, gets angry, and *apologizes to the merchant anyway.* The proof of concept, rolling home on a cart.
- **Khaeros the minotaur** — a Thyatian-born captive who escaped a slave ship bound for the Black Eagle. Wealthy, armored, trained, "attacks first" — because the last time he didn't, he was chained. His hoard is unspent passage money: he has the means to go home and not the map, and home would call him a monster anyway. Karameikos can offer the one thing his birthplace won't — to be recognized as a person. The exile choosing whether *standing among strangers* outweighs *belonging among his own*.
- **The chiefs** — who, when asked to march on the cult, decline: *that's a human problem.* Their refusal is the federation behaving like a state — assessing a threat and declining to bleed for it. The caves have foreign policy now.

## The challenge underneath the challenge

Broker the peace and you remove the caves' food source, because the raiding *was* the food source. Tolerance without trade is just a slower death — and the hungrier the caves get, the better the cult's recruiting. So the real campaign isn't diplomacy. It's **political economy:** build a borderland that can feed seven hungry polities before the stolen gold runs out, by finding what each can produce and who will buy it. Tribute breeds resentment; reframe it as *payroll* — food for patrol work, for holding ground the Duke can't garrison — and the hardliner in the Keep can vote yes without losing face.

## The thing in the basement

One evil stays solid, because a frame that explains everything explains nothing. At the back of the shunned caverns is a shrine to **Thanatos**, Hierarch of the Sphere of Entropy — older than the Traladaran faith, older than Halav, Petra, and Zirchev (who arose to *hold him back*). He cannot be traded with, because he wants what everyone has and offers nothing: the end of all wanting.

And the cult is not what it seems, in layers:
- It wears a **Traladaran liberation** mask — overthrow the Thyatian colonizers — and that grievance is *real.* The conquest was illegitimate. But the cult is older than the grievance; the grievance is just this century's costume.
- The famine is **manufactured.** The undead destroy the caves' food at night, starving them into raids on the Keep, bleeding the frontier. The chiefs blame the humans and the land; the misdiagnosis *is* the corruption working.
- The cultists don't know they're a node of the **Iron Ring** — Bargle's organization, run out of the **Black Eagle Barony.** They believe they fight the colonizer. They serve the worst colonizer of all. Their rage is real and aimed exactly wrong.

So the most devastating weapon against the cult isn't the sword — it's the **truth**, which only the suppressed **Church of Traladar** can deliver with legitimacy, because the state **Church of Karameikos** (the fusion faith that absorbed Halav into a tidy "virtue" and forgot what he was *for*) would cleanse the shrine while manufacturing the next generation's martyrs. The empire blinded itself by assimilating the only people who could still see the enemy.

## The vertical: three cities, three kinds of power, one pipeline

The frontier is the whole society in miniature. Follow the iron-ring evidence and the same rot wears a different face at every altitude:

| Scale | Power as | Function | The denial |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Caves of Chaos** | strength & hunger | capture (intake) | "the cult is a *human* problem, not ours" |
| **Specularum** | blood & wall | shipping (the port) | the Duke won't believe the rumors about his own baron |
| **Darokin City** | money & guild | laundering (the market) | the republic won't trace the goods — it would threaten the credit the whole city runs on |

The maps wear it openly. Specularum's poor are labeled **The Nest**; Darokin files its citizens by metal — **Gold Class, Silver Class, Copper Class** — and zones them by riverbank. Nobody had to invent the stratification. It's printed on the cartography.

## The one skill the whole campaign teaches

From an LA-apartment-complex reading of a monster dungeon to a slave-laundering operation in the world's proudest free-trade republic, the connective tissue is a single act of perception:

**Look at who got pushed to the bottom, and follow the money to whoever profits from keeping them there.**

The campaign opens with a dungeon you clear with a sword and ends with a ledger you can only defeat by making someone with power finally look at the numbers. The hardest evil to fight is never the one that looks like a monster. It's the one that looks like virtue.

### Notes for running it

- **Keep the controls solid.** The temple must be genuinely irredeemable, and a couple of individuals (the medusa, the evil human "Hero" in the bugbear pens) must be exactly as dangerous as their stat blocks say. Without them, "monsters are misunderstood" collapses into a different flatness — "monsters are secretly nice." Some aren't. One human here is worse than most of the caves.
- **Don't let the conspiracy eat the intimate story.** The Iron Ring is the horizon, not the whole sky. The ogre buying a bed and the minotaur's unspent passage money are the heart; the national plot is the dark root the heart keeps hitting.
- **Let victories curdle on a delay.** The party will probably just go in with clerics and kill the cult. Fine. Salt the temple with evidence they carry out without understanding, and let the Church of Traladar — noticing the symbols that *don't* match real Thanatos worship — turn a clean win into the cold realization, weeks later, that they touched the edge of something national.
- **The win condition is generational, not final.** You can defeat any *instance* of Thanatos's work. You cannot kill the patient thing itself. The ending isn't "the evil is destroyed." It's "the evil is *named and held,* and the living have remembered, for now, that their real enemies were never each other."

*Setting: Grand Duchy of Karameikos, Mystara (BECMI / Known World). Built on B2: The Keep on the Borderlands, with the Iron Ring, the Church of Karameikos / Church of Traladar, and the Sphere of Entropy.*
